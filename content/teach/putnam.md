## What the exam is and where the points are

From 2026 the exam has a new shape: four sessions of 90 minutes on one Saturday, A, B, C and D, with three problems each, A1 to A3 through D1 to D3. Until 2025 it was two three-hour sessions of six, A1 to A6 and B1 to B6, and every past paper named in this doc has that older shape. The total is unchanged: twelve problems, each scored out of 10. In Eastern time the sessions start at 11:00, 12:45, 16:00 and 17:45, so the breaks are fifteen minutes, an hour and three quarters, and fifteen minutes, and the day ends at 19:15. The policy does not say how difficulty is spread within a session; first is easiest is likely and unconfirmed. Almost every score has been 0, 1, 9 or 10: a complete argument with a small gap gets 9, an idea without a proof gets 1 or 0. Under the old shape the median total most years was a single digit, one fully solved problem put you above the median and two put you in the top quarter; the thresholds for the new shape are not known.

That sets the strategy. The points are in finishing rather than starting, and in the approachable problems, which in the old papers were A1, A2, B1 and B2. Across 2016 to 2025 the forty problems in those four slots carry these tags: congruences and number theory 13, sums, integrals and limits 10, counting and configurations 10, polynomials and roots 9, geometry 6, inequalities 5, with linear algebra, recurrences, pigeonhole and invariants at 3 each and probability, games, generating functions, functional equations and abstract algebra almost never before A3. The four ladders below follow that order. That each of the four new sessions opens with a problem of that kind is a guess; the tag counts are what carries over without guessing. The target is two clean solutions across the four sessions.

## Reading a problem, and the call

A Putnam problem is the analogy puzzle at scale: find the relevant information, induce the rule, apply it. Before any technique, three questions. What is the object: an integer, a polynomial, a function, a sequence, a matrix, a set of points, a game position. What is asked: show one exists, show it holds for all, find all, compute a value, decide yes or no. What is the smallest case: n of 1, 2 and 3, the zero polynomial, the empty set, a 2 by 2 matrix, written out by hand. The small cases name the technique more often than the statement does, and they are the cheapest work you can do.

Then the call line, in calls.md, domain math:

    math: 2021 A1, pigeonhole on the hop lengths, assume the answer is the obvious lower bound plus a parity fix, 25 min. 55

The technique, the first assumption, the time, the confidence. Then work. When the box runs out or the problem is done, open the solution in the archive and settle in the same sitting: held if the technique you named carried it; missed if a different one from your list did; blind if the solution uses something you did not consider. A solved problem is still settled honestly; solving it by a route you did not name is a miss. When a later problem is carried by a technique you called earlier, write the earlier id in the call ("as in c12"), which is how the readout sees the transfer.

Time boxes: 25 minutes for an A1, A2, B1, B2 or a seminar problem; 45 for A3, B3 or anything marked harder. The box is not patience. It is the point after which the solution teaches you more than the struggle does, and the call line has already captured what the struggle was for.

Four sessions, Eastern time: A 11:00 to 12:30, B 12:45 to 14:15, C 16:00 to 17:30, D 17:45 to 19:15. ## Hidden independence and uniformity

Recognise it when a problem has a symmetry it does not mention: points chosen at random, a sum over all permutations, a quantity that should not depend on a choice that was made, a sign pattern that pairs off. The model: find the random variable, or the group action, under which the thing asked for is invariant, then compute in the easiest representative. Uniformity means every element of a symmetric set is equally likely or equally weighted, so an average over the set equals the value at any one element; independence means a joint probability factors, which lets a hard count become a product of easy ones. First moves: pair the terms (k with n minus k, a permutation with its inverse, a point with its reflection); ask whether the answer depends on the parameter at all; compute the smallest case and see if it is suspiciously clean. Traps: an independence that looks obvious and is false, which the smallest case usually exposes; a pairing that leaves a fixed point unpaired. Ladder: 2018 B1, 2020 B1, 2019 A4, 2021 A4, 2017 A6, 2023 B3. Seminar: PS 1, whose preface says every problem in it has an elegant solution of this kind; start with #1, #2, #3, #12, #20; #11(d) is flagged hard. Where it hides: inside number theory (2020 B1) and probability (2023 B3).

## Pigeonhole and extremal arguments

Pigeonhole: more objects than boxes forces two in one box. Recognise it when a problem asks to show two things coincide, that some difference is divisible, that a pattern repeats, or that something exists with no construction in sight. The work is choosing the boxes: residues modulo n, intervals of length 1 over n, subsets by size or by largest odd divisor. The extremal argument: look at the largest, smallest, first or last object, and ask what being extreme forces; a minimal counterexample that can be made smaller is a contradiction. First moves: the obvious boxes before anything cleverer; for existence with a bound, ask what the extreme object looks like. Traps: counting the boxes wrong by one; an extremal object that need not exist (an infinite set with no largest element). Ladder: Pigeonhole #1, #6, #7, #8 (the two classic "among n plus 1 numbers in 1 to 2n" facts), 2021 A1, 2023 B2, 2022 A2, 2025 B5, 2025 A5, 2024 A3, 2016 B3. Seminar: the Pigeonhole set; #9 is noted as much harder and not really pigeonhole; #17(a) is Fermat's classic. Where it hides: number theory, as a bound on a count of residues.

## Invariants and monovariants

An invariant is a quantity a process leaves unchanged; a monovariant is one it moves in one direction. Recognise them when a problem describes moves, operations, a game, a recolouring, and asks whether a state is reachable or whether the process stops. The model: find a number, often a parity, a sum or a product modulo something, a colouring count, that every move preserves, and compare its value at the start and at the target; for termination, find a quantity that strictly decreases in a well-ordered set, usually the non-negative integers. For reachability in both directions, an invariant proves the negative and a construction proves the positive, and a complete answer needs both. First moves: parity of a sum; the number of objects on black squares; the sum of positions. Traps: a quantity preserved by most moves and not by one; proving the invariant and forgetting the construction for the reachable states. Ladder: 2023 B1, 2024 B1, 2017 A4, 2022 B3, 2020 B2, 2025 A3. Seminar: PS 10 #13, #14, #27. Where it hides: games, where the invariant is the winning position's signature.

## Induction

Recognise it when the statement has a natural number in it and the n case is built from smaller ones. Ordinary induction uses n minus 1, strong induction uses every smaller case, and a two-variable statement sometimes wants induction on one variable with the other fixed. The model choice inside induction is the strengthening: the statement as given is often too weak to pass from n minus 1 to n, and a stronger claim, with an extra term or a tighter bound, is what the step needs. First moves: compute cases to 4; try the step on the statement as given; when it fails, ask what fact about n minus 1 you wished you had, and add it to the claim. Traps: a base case that is not the one the step starts from; a step that silently uses n at least 2. Ladder: 2017 A1, PS 4 #1, #3, #5, 2016 A5. Where it hides: recurrences (every closed form guessed from the first terms is proved by it) and inequalities (Bernoulli-type products).

## Inequalities

Recognise them when a problem asks to bound a sum, product or integral, or to find the largest or smallest value of an expression, or to show a function stays on one side of another. The three results that carry most Putnam inequalities: the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality (the arithmetic mean of positive numbers is at least their geometric mean, with equality only when all are equal); Cauchy-Schwarz (the square of a sum of products is at most the product of the two sums of squares, with equality when the sequences are proportional); and convexity through Jensen (for a convex function, the function of an average is at most the average of the function). For functions, the tangent line of a convex function lies below it, and comparing derivatives at a shared point settles which of two functions is larger near it. The equality case tells you which inequality the problem was built from. First moves: find where equality should hold and check the claimed extreme there; for a sum of n terms with a symmetric constraint, try all terms equal; for a function on an interval, check the endpoints and the stationary points. Traps: applying an inequality in the direction that gives a bound the wrong way; an extreme at the boundary rather than at the symmetric point; a convex function assumed where the second derivative changes sign. Ladder: PS 5 #3, #4, #13, 2025 A2, 2018 B2, 2021 B2, 2018 A3, 2019 A3, 2017 A3, 2025 B4. Seminar: PS 5 (Hardy, Carleman, Hilbert and Kantorovich types appear by shape in #1, #2, #6, #9). Where it hides: inside limits, as the bound that squeezes.

## Polynomials and their roots

Recognise them when a polynomial is given by a property of its roots or its values rather than by its coefficients, or when an expression in several variables is symmetric. The model: Vieta's formulas (the coefficients are the elementary symmetric functions of the roots); the factor theorem (a root r is a factor x minus r); degree counting (a polynomial of degree n with more than n roots is the zero polynomial, so two polynomials agreeing at n plus 1 points are equal); Rolle's theorem for real roots (between two real roots of p there is a root of p prime); and the roots of unity for anything cyclic or for extracting every k-th coefficient. Lagrange interpolation builds the polynomial from its values, and the finite difference of order n of a degree n polynomial is a constant. First moves: write the polynomial by its roots, not its coefficients; evaluate at 0, 1, minus 1 and the roots of unity; take a derivative; reduce the coefficients modulo 2. Traps: roots that are complex or repeated; a polynomial identity checked at finitely many points without counting degrees; forgetting the leading coefficient when writing by roots. Ladder: 2019 A1, 2023 A2, Roots #6, Roots #7, 2016 A1, 2022 B1, 2024 A2, 2017 A2, 2019 B5, 2018 B2, 2019 A3, 2022 A2. Seminar: Roots #1, #8, #11a, with #11(b) noted as considerably harder; #23 is the Chebyshev bound, #25 Descartes's rule. Where it hides: recurrences (2017 A2, 2019 B5), congruences (2016 A1, 2022 B1).

## Congruences and number theory

Recognise them when a problem is about integers, divisibility, remainders, digits, primes, or when a polynomial or recurrence is asked about modulo something. The model: arithmetic modulo n (the integers collapse to n classes and addition and multiplication respect the collapse); Fermat's little theorem (a to the p minus 1 is 1 modulo a prime p not dividing a) and Euler's generalisation to any modulus coprime to a; the order of an element (the smallest exponent returning 1, which divides every exponent that does, and so divides p minus 1); the smallest-prime-factor argument (take the smallest prime dividing n and look at orders modulo it); the Chinese remainder theorem (congruences modulo coprime moduli solve together); the lifting of solutions from modulo p to modulo p squared; and the exponent of a prime in n factorial. For Diophantine equations: factor, bound, reduce modulo 3, 4, 5, 7, 8 and 9 until the cases die. First moves: the smallest cases by hand; reduce modulo 4 and modulo 3; factor anything that factors; look at the order. Traps: dividing a congruence by something not coprime to the modulus; a modulus that kills the information you need; forgetting that 2 is prime. Ladder: 2018 A1, 2017 B2, PS 9 #14, #15, 2020 A1, 2024 A1, 2021 A1, 2023 B2, 2018 B3, 2024 A4, 2021 A5, 2025 B3. Seminar: PS 9, with #1, #4, #12, #31 as warm-ups and #16, #17, #19 for power towers; Floor #2 is the exponent of a prime in a factorial, Floor #12 to #15 are Putnam floor problems with their score statistics printed. Where it hides: polynomials (week 2), the binary digits of a count (2020 B1), games with integers (2023 A6).

## Recurrences

Recognise them when a sequence is defined from its earlier terms, or when a count for n splits by what happens first, which builds it from counts for smaller n. The model: a linear recurrence with constant coefficients is solved by its characteristic polynomial (the roots are the ratios of the geometric sequences the solution is a combination of, with a factor of n for a repeated root); a recurrence that looks nonlinear often linearises by a substitution (a ratio, a logarithm, a tangent); a closed form guessed from the first terms is proved by induction; and a sequence modulo m is eventually periodic, because there are finitely many states. First moves: compute six terms and look at them (Fibonacci, Catalan, powers and factorials in disguise are common); compute the differences; compute modulo small numbers. Traps: a repeated characteristic root; a recurrence whose initial terms break the pattern; assuming periodicity starts at the first term. Ladder: 2016 B1, PS 4 #1, #3, #5, #9, 2017 A2, 2025 A1, 2020 A3, 2018 B4, 2021 B4, 2019 B5. Seminar: PS 4, with #21(c) and #22 marked very hard; #15 is the Stern sequence. Where it hides: counting (2020 A5), probability (2020 A4, 2020 B3, 2024 B4), number theory (2025 A6).

## Sums, integrals and limits

Recognise them when a sum or integral has to be evaluated or bounded, or a limit found, or a sequence shown to converge. The model: telescoping (write the term as f of k plus 1 minus f of k so the sum collapses); swapping the order of a double sum or integral, which is allowed when the double sum converges absolutely (Fubini) or every term is non-negative (Tonelli); the Riemann sum (a sum of n terms with a 1 over n in front, each term a function of k over n, is an integral in the limit); comparison of a sum with an integral to bound it; logarithms to turn a product into a sum; and the first surviving term of an expansion (for a limit of the form something to the power of something, take the logarithm, expand, keep the first term that does not cancel). For convergence: squeeze between two things you can compute; a monotone bounded sequence converges; a sequence with a contraction step converges. First moves: write the first three terms of the sum out; look for a difference; put a 1 over n in front and see if it is a Riemann sum; take a logarithm. Traps: swapping an order of summation when the series is only conditionally convergent; a Riemann sum whose endpoints are off by one term that matters; a limit taken inside an integral without a reason (dominated or monotone convergence). Ladder: Limits #15, Limits #12, 2016 B1, 2022 A1, 2019 B2, 2021 A2, 2020 A2, 2024 B3, 2023 A1, 2017 B4, 2016 B6. Seminar: PS 2 (the notes before its problems state Fubini, Tonelli, dominated and monotone convergence, Feynman's differentiation under the integral sign, and the trick that turns a denominator into an integral; the worked example is 2016 B6), PS 3 for analysis proper, the Limits set in two parts, the second entirely from old Putnams. Where it hides: inequalities (the squeeze), probability (an expectation is a sum).

## Probability and expectation

Recognise it when a problem says random, expected, probability, or when a counting problem can be read as one by dividing by the total. The model: linearity of expectation (the expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values, with no independence needed), which reduces most Putnam probability to writing the quantity as a sum of indicator variables (1 if an event happens, 0 if not) and adding their probabilities; symmetry, which is the previous section; and conditioning on the first step, which turns a probability into a recurrence. For geometric probability, the probability is a ratio of areas or volumes, and the picture is the whole problem. First moves: indicators; the first step; the smallest case with all outcomes listed. Traps: multiplying probabilities of events that share randomness; a sample space that is not uniform (two dice summing to 7 is not one outcome). Ladder: PS 6 #3, #5, #13, 2021 B1, 2020 A4, 2020 B3, 2023 B3, 2024 B4, 2022 A4, 2016 B4. Seminar: PS 6, with #24 to #28 a subsection on probability games. Where it hides: counting, by the probabilistic method (a random object works with positive probability, so one exists).

## Linear algebra

Recognise it when matrices, determinants, ranks or vector spaces appear, or when a problem about numbers or functions has a hidden vector space: polynomials of degree at most n, functions on a finite set, sequences satisfying a recurrence. The model: rank and nullity (the dimension of the image plus the dimension of the kernel is the dimension of the source); eigenvalues (a matrix acts as a scalar on an eigenvector; the trace is the sum of the eigenvalues and the determinant their product; a real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis); the determinant as a multilinear alternating function of the rows, so row operations do not change it and a repeated row kills it; and a matrix over the integers modulo 2 for anything with lamps, switches or parity. First moves: compute the 2 by 2 and 3 by 3 cases; look at the trace and determinant; ask what the kernel is. Traps: a matrix that is not diagonalisable; a determinant computed by expansion when row operations would do it in two lines; forgetting that the dimension argument needs linear independence proved. Ladder: PS 7 #1, #7, #9, 2018 A2, 2019 B3, 2022 B2, 2017 B1, 2021 B5, 2025 A4. Seminar: PS 7, sectioned basic, determinants, eigenvalues, combinatorics, miscellaneous; #28 grades its own parts from easy to even more difficult. Where it hides: combinatorics (2018 A2 is a determinant of an intersection matrix), geometry (2017 B1).

## Counting and configurations

Recognise them when a problem is about arrangements, colourings, tilings, subsets with a property, lattice points, or a graph described as people and acquaintances. The model: double counting (count the same set two ways and equate, which is how most "show the number is even" or "show two things are equal" problems go); bijection (match the objects to a set you can count); complementary counting; the probabilistic method; graph language (points and lines become vertices and edges, and the question becomes degrees, cycles or matchings); and the colouring argument for tilings (colour the board so each tile covers a fixed pattern, then count). First moves: list every object for n of 2 and 3; colour the board; count by rows and by columns and compare. Traps: overcounting symmetric arrangements; a bijection that is not onto; a colouring that proves impossibility and forgets the construction for the possible cases. Ladder: PS 10 #1, #4, #7, 2018 B1, 2019 B1, 2023 B1, 2024 B1, 2017 A4, 2022 B4, 2016 A4, 2025 A5, 2024 A3. Seminar: PS 10, whose preface says it imitates recent Putnam combinatorics with difficulty rising roughly and the first several exercise-like. Where it hides: everywhere; a count is often what a number theory or probability problem reduces to.

## Generating functions

Recognise them when a sequence or a count has a convolution in it, meaning the n-th term depends on sums of products of earlier terms; when a recurrence has polynomial coefficients; or when a sum of binomial coefficients with a pattern is asked for. The model: encode the sequence as the coefficients of a power series, turn the recurrence into an equation for the series, solve it, and read the coefficients back; a product of series is a convolution of sequences, which is why they fit counting problems that split an object into parts; the ordinary generating function for unlabelled objects and the exponential one (divide by n factorial) for labelled ones; roots of unity to extract every k-th coefficient. First moves: write the series; multiply by x and subtract; find the closed form; partial fractions. Traps: convergence is irrelevant for formal power series and relevant the moment you evaluate at a number; a recurrence that starts late, so the first terms need their own correction. Ladder: PS 11 #2, #3, #7, 2017 B3, 2023 A5, 2024 A6, 2020 B4. Seminar: PS 11, whose preface says only generating-function solutions earn credit there. Where it hides: recurrences and counting, as the method that does the bookkeeping.

## Abstract algebra

Recognise it when a problem gives a set with an operation and some rules, or asks about functions that respect a structure, or when a number theory problem is really about the multiplicative group modulo n. The model: Lagrange's theorem (the order of an element divides the order of the group); a group of prime order is cyclic; the multiplicative group modulo a prime is cyclic; a homomorphism is determined by where it sends generators; in a finite cancellative structure, left multiplication is a bijection. First moves: identify the structure as one you know in disguise; look for the identity and inverses; count elements by their orders. Traps: assuming commutativity; assuming associativity when the problem gives a bare binary operation. Ladder: PS 8 #1, #2, #3, 2016 A5, 2018 A4, 2023 B5. Seminar: PS 8, the only set that prints its Putnam sources, 1972 to 2010 for #1 to #15; #21, #24 and #25 marked difficult. Where it hides: congruences (2023 B5, 2021 A5), permutations.

## Functional equations

Recognise them when a function is defined by an equation it satisfies for all inputs, or when a function is characterised by a relation between its values at related points. The model: substitute special values (0, 1, x equal to y, y equal to minus x, y equal to 1 over x) to pin down values; look for the substitution that makes two terms cancel; guess the form (constant, linear, a power, an exponential) from the special values and prove it is the only one; ask whether the equation forces injectivity or surjectivity, which then allows cancelling; for a continuous function, prove the form on the rationals and extend. First moves: f of 0; x equal to y; the guess. Traps: proving the guess satisfies the equation and forgetting uniqueness; dividing by f of something that might be zero; using continuity you were not given. Ladder: PS 3 #8, 2022 B6, 2016 A3, 2016 B5, 2025 B6. Where it hides: analysis (2016 A3 asks for an integral), inequalities (2025 B6).

## Geometry

Recognise it when points, lines, circles, triangles, polygons or lattice points appear. It takes 6 of the 40 easy slots in the last decade, so it needs a place even though the seminar has no set for it. The model: coordinates (put the figure on axes, with the symmetric choice of origin, and compute), complex numbers for anything with rotations or regular polygons, vectors for anything with midpoints and centroids, and the classical facts (the centroid divides a median 2 to 1, the incentre is where the angle bisectors meet, the inscribed angle is half the central angle). For lattice points, parity and the equation of the circle or line in integers. For counting or existence, the pigeonhole on the plane (divide the region into pieces small enough). First moves: draw it to scale; coordinates with the origin at the symmetric point; the smallest case. Traps: a configuration drawn in a special position that the problem did not promise; a sign lost in a coordinate. Ladder: 2019 A2, 2017 B1, 2019 B1, 2021 B1, 2024 B2, 2025 B1, 2021 A3, 2016 B3. Where it hides: counting (lattice points), probability (geometric probability).

## Games

Recognise them when two players alternate moves and the question is who wins, or what one player can guarantee. The model: a winning position is one from which some move leads to a losing position, a losing position is one from which every move leads to a winning one, and the small cases computed backwards from the end usually reveal the pattern; a pairing strategy (answer each move with its mirror) proves a win by symmetry; an invariant that the second player can restore is a second-player win. First moves: compute the positions from the end for the smallest board; look for a pairing. Traps: a pairing broken by a central element; a strategy that works for the first player only if the game ends. Ladder: 2020 B2, 2022 A5, 2023 A6, 2025 A3, 2017 A5. Seminar: PS 6 #24 to #28 for probability games, PS 10 #8, #13, #14, #18 for configuration games. Where it hides: invariants, which is the same section seen from the second player's side.
