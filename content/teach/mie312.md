## The Reynolds transport theorem

A system is a fixed lump of matter, and the laws of physics are written for systems. A control volume is a region of space you choose, and problems are solved on control volumes. The theorem translates between them. The rate of change of a property of the system equals the rate of change inside the control volume plus the net outflow through its surface. For a moving control volume, use the velocity relative to the surface. The sign comes from V·n, with n the outward normal: outflow counts positive, inflow negative. Every law in Chapter 3 is this theorem with a different property put in: mass, momentum, angular momentum or energy. The sheet writes the property per unit mass as β; the PDF writes b, to keep β for the momentum correction factor.

## Conservation of mass

Inflow minus outflow equals the rate of change of mass inside. When the flow is steady, mass in equals mass out. When it is also incompressible, volume flow in equals volume flow out. The mean velocity is the volume flow divided by the area. When the profile is not uniform, integrate it over the area. A filling or draining tank gives an ODE in the level h: the area at height h times dh/dt equals Q in minus Q out. Separate it and integrate. Signs and the area as a function of h are where marks go.

## Linear momentum for a control volume

The sum of forces on the control volume equals the rate of change of momentum inside, plus momentum out, minus momentum in. The flux at each port is the mass flow times the velocity there, taken as a vector. Forces include pressure at every cut, gravity, and the support force you want. Atmospheric pressure acting all round a closed surface cancels, so use gauge pressures. The force at a free jet is then zero. For a nonuniform profile, multiply the flux by β: 4/3 for laminar pipe flow, about 1.02 for turbulent.

A vane moving at speed u sees the relative speed V minus u. A single vane receives mass at ρA(V − u). A wheel of vanes receives the full ρAV. A single vane's best power is at u = V/3, a wheel's at V/2.

## Bernoulli and the grade lines

Along a streamline, in steady, incompressible, frictionless flow with no shaft work and no heat, p/ρg + V²/2g + z is constant. It follows from F = ma on a particle moving along the streamline. Stagnation pressure is static pressure plus ½ρV². A Pitot-static tube gives V = √(2Δp/ρ). A hole in a tank gives Torricelli's √(2gh). A siphon's summit pressure is limited by the vapour pressure. The hydraulic grade line is z + p/γ, and the energy grade line sits αV²/2g above it. Bernoulli is the wrong model across a pump or turbine, where there is friction, across streamlines in rotational flow, and in unsteady flow.

## Angular momentum

The sum of moments about a point equals the net outflow of r × V times the mass flow, plus the rate of change inside. Moments come from pipe bends, arms and sprinklers. For a rotating arm, the absolute jet velocity is the relative velocity plus ωr. A free sprinkler spins until the absolute tangential velocity of its jets is zero. Euler's turbomachine equation follows directly: torque equals the mass flow times the change in rVt, power equals torque times ω, and the head is (U₂Vt₂ − U₁Vt₁)/g. That equation is on the sheet in section 14.

## The energy equation

This is the first law for a control volume, written in heads, meaning energy per unit weight in metres:

p₁/γ + α₁V₁²/2g + z₁ + h_pump = p₂/γ + α₂V₂²/2g + z₂ + h_turbine + h_loss

α is 2 for laminar pipe flow and about 1.05 for turbulent. Power is ρgQh. Pump input power is ρgQh_p divided by the efficiency. Turbine output is ρgQh_t times the efficiency. The head loss is the irreversibility, friction turned into heat. Energy is Bernoulli plus a pump, minus a turbine, minus losses.

## The acceleration field

Fluid velocity is a field V(x, y, z, t). A particle's acceleration is the material derivative, DV/Dt = ∂V/∂t + (V·∇)V. That is the local change at a fixed point plus the convective change from moving to a place with a different velocity. The chain rule gives it. A steady flow can still accelerate particles, as through a nozzle. Two ways to fail: forget the convective terms, or evaluate at the wrong point. The sheet does not print this.

## Differential continuity

A mass balance on a small box gives ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρV) = 0. For incompressible flow it becomes ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y + ∂w/∂z = 0. The sheet prints only the 2-D form. The cylindrical form is (1/r)∂(r v_r)/∂r + (1/r)∂v_θ/∂θ + ∂v_z/∂z = 0. To find a missing component, integrate. The "constant" is a function of the other variables, fixed by a boundary condition. A field that fails continuity is not physically possible.

## The Navier-Stokes equations

Newton's law on a particle: ρDV/Dt = −∇p + ρg + μ∇²V, for an incompressible Newtonian fluid of constant viscosity. Each component reads mass times acceleration equals the pressure push, plus gravity, plus the viscous diffusion of that component. The Laplacian ∇² is the sum of the second derivatives. The PDF derives it from the stresses on a box. It also gives the cylindrical forms, with a check for their extra terms: rigid rotation and a line source must make the viscous term vanish.

Euler's equation is the frictionless case, μ = 0. A common question gives a velocity field and asks for the pressure: rearrange each component for ∂p/∂x and the others, then integrate. Not on the sheet. The PDF has a five-step recipe to write it from nothing.

## Boundary conditions

- At a wall, the fluid moves with the wall (no slip) and does not pass through it (no penetration), unless the wall is porous.
- At a free surface, the pressure is atmospheric and the shear is zero, unless wind drags on it.
- At an interface between two liquids, the velocity and the shear stress are continuous.
- At a symmetry plane, the derivative across it is zero.
- On the axis in cylindrical coordinates, the velocity must be finite. That kills any ln r term in a pipe, though not in an annulus.

The sheet prints only no-slip.

## Vorticity and irrotationality

Vorticity is the curl of the velocity, ζ = ∇ × V. In 2-D, ζ_z = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y. A fluid element spins at half the vorticity. A flow with zero vorticity everywhere is irrotational, and only then does Bernoulli hold across streamlines. Rigid rotation has vorticity 2ω everywhere. A free vortex, with v_θ = K/r, is irrotational except at its centre. Every viscous shear flow near a wall, Couette included, is rotational. The sheet prints the 2-D definitions.

## Exact viscous flows

The procedure is the call: list the assumptions (steady, incompressible, fully developed, parallel, 2-D or axisymmetric, gravity kept or not). Continuity then shows which velocities vanish. Strike terms in Navier-Stokes with a reason for each, integrate twice, and apply the boundary conditions. The PDF derives six flows:

- Couette: linear profile.
- Plane Poiseuille: a parabola. Q per width is −(dp/dx)·(2h)³/(12μ) for a channel of gap 2h.
- Couette with a pressure gradient.
- Hagen-Poiseuille in a pipe: a parabola, mean speed half the maximum, Q = πR⁴(−dp/dz)/(8μ). That is the sheet's πD⁴ΔP/(128μL), and it gives f = 64/Re.
- A film down an incline: zero shear at the free surface.
- Flow between rotating cylinders: v_θ = Ar + B/r.

The wall shear is μ times the slope of the profile at the wall. Drag and torque come from integrating it.

## Dimensional homogeneity

Every valid physical equation has the same dimensions in every term. The primary dimensions are mass M, length L, time T, and temperature Θ. Get each quantity's dimensions from its definition. Force is MLT⁻², pressure ML⁻¹T⁻², dynamic viscosity ML⁻¹T⁻¹, kinematic viscosity L²T⁻¹, surface tension MT⁻², power ML²T⁻³. The sheet's section 16 table lists these. It does not list flow rate, mass flow, angular velocity, energy, specific heat or bulk modulus. An empirical formula with a dimensional constant only works in the units it was written in.

## The Pi theorem

n variables with dimensional matrix rank r give n − r independent dimensionless groups. The method has six steps.

1. List the variables.
2. Write their dimensions.
3. Find the rank. That is the largest set of rows that cannot be combined into one another, and it can be less than the number of dimensions.
4. Choose r repeating variables that are dimensionally independent and do not include the one you want to predict.
5. Combine each other variable with them into a group, solving for the exponents.
6. Check each group, then rename into Re, Fr, We, Ma, Eu and St.

A different choice of repeating variables changes the form of the groups but not their number. One group means the group is a constant, which is a scaling law.

## Nondimensional equations

Scale every variable in Navier-Stokes and continuity by a reference length, speed, time and pressure. The coefficients that remain are the groups: Re in front of the viscous term, Fr with gravity, Eu with pressure, St with the unsteady term. Large Re means viscosity matters only near walls. Small Re means creeping flow, where pressure balances viscosity and inertia drops out (a sphere's C_D = 24/Re). A free surface brings in Fr, and with surface tension We. The sheet lists the groups but not which term each multiplies.

## Modelling and similarity

Similarity has three levels:

- Geometric: same shape, every length in one ratio.
- Kinematic: velocities in one ratio.
- Dynamic: forces in one ratio, which means every Pi group matches.

Then scale force as ρV²L², power as ρV³L², and time as L/V. Reynolds matching is for enclosed and immersed flows. Froude matching is for ships, spillways and waves, where speed scales as √L, force as L³ and power as L^3.5 in the same fluid. Mach matching is for compressible flow. Froude and Reynolds conflict in a water model of a water prototype. The usual way out is to accept the Reynolds mismatch when the flow is in a range where Re barely matters. Ship tanks instead split the drag into a friction part and a wave part and scale each separately. A model test is the wrong tool when the groups cannot be matched and the mismatched one matters.
