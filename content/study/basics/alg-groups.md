## Groups

> [!def] Group
> A **group** is a set $G$ with an operation assigning to each ordered pair $a, b$ an element $ab \in G$, such that: $(ab)c = a(bc)$ always (associativity); there is $e \in G$ with $ea = ae = a$ for all $a$ (an identity); each $a$ has an $a^{-1}$ with $aa^{-1} = a^{-1}a = e$ (an inverse). $G$ is **abelian** if $ab = ba$ always. The **order** $|G|$ is its number of elements. Abelian groups are often written with $+$, $0$ and $-a$.
^group-def

Examples: the integers under $+$; residues mod $n$ under $+$; $U(n)$ under $\times$ ([units](nt-congruence.md#^unit-gcd)), of order $\varphi(n)$; the nonzero elements of a [field](alg-polynomials.md#^field-def) under $\times$; the permutations of $\{1, \dots, n\}$ under composition, of order $n!$ and not abelian for $n \ge 3$. Non-examples: all residues mod $6$ under $\times$ ($2$ has no inverse); the integers under subtraction (not associative). ^group-examples

> [!thm] Proposition 1 (basic rules)
> In a group the identity and each inverse are unique; $ax = ay$ forces $x = y$ and $xa = ya$ forces $x = y$; $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$; and for fixed $a$ the map $x \mapsto ax$ is a bijection of $G$.
^group-basics

> [!proof]- Proof
> 1. Two identities: $e = ee' = e'$.
> 2. Two inverses $b, c$ of $a$: $b = be = b(ac) = (ba)c = ec = c$.
> 3. Multiply $ax = ay$ on the left by $a^{-1}$; the other side likewise.
> 4. $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = a(bb^{-1})a^{-1} = e$, and the other order likewise.
> 5. $x \mapsto ax$ is injective by step 3 and onto, since $y = a(a^{-1}y)$.
^p-group-basics

Powers: $g^0 = e$, $g^{m+1} = g^m g$, $g^{-m} = (g^{-1})^m$. Induction gives $g^m g^k = g^{m+k}$ and $(g^m)^k = g^{mk}$ for all integers $m, k$. The rule $(ab)^m = a^m b^m$ needs $ab = ba$. ^powers

## Subgroups and order

> [!def] Subgroup, cyclic group, order of an element
> A **subgroup** of $G$ is a subset $H$ containing $e$ and closed under products and inverses; it is a group with the same operation. For $g \in G$, $\langle g \rangle = \{g^m : m \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is the **cyclic subgroup** generated by $g$, and $G$ is **cyclic** if $G = \langle g \rangle$ for some $g$, a **generator**. The **order** $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ is the least $d \ge 1$ with $g^d = e$, if there is one. For a unit $a$ mod $n$, $\operatorname{ord}_n(a)$ is its order in $U(n)$.
^subgroup

> [!thm] Theorem 2 (order of an element)
> In a finite group every element has an order. If $\operatorname{ord}(g) = d$, then $g^m = e$ exactly when $d \mid m$; $g^m = g^k$ exactly when $m \equiv k \pmod d$; $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, \dots, g^{d-1}\}$ has exactly $d$ elements; and $\operatorname{ord}(g^k) = d/\gcd(d, k)$.
^elt-order

> [!proof]- Proof (d 2.5)
> 1. In a finite group the powers $g, g^2, \dots$ repeat: $g^i = g^j$ with $i < j$, and cancelling gives $g^{j-i} = e$. So a least such exponent $d$ exists.
> 2. Write $m = qd + r$ with $0 \le r < d$. Then $g^m = (g^d)^q g^r = g^r$, and $g^r = e$ with $0 \le r < d$ only for $r = 0$, by minimality.
> 3. $g^m = g^k$ exactly when $g^{m-k} = e$, that is $d \mid m - k$. So the powers are $g^0, \dots, g^{d-1}$, all different.
> 4. $(g^k)^j = e$ exactly when $d \mid kj$, which by cancelling is $\frac{d}{\gcd(d,k)} \mid j$. The least such $j \ge 1$ is $d/\gcd(d, k)$.
^p-elt-order

> [!thm] Theorem 3 (Lagrange)
> If $H$ is a subgroup of a finite group $G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$. The **left cosets** $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$ split $G$ into $|G|/|H|$ disjoint pieces of size $|H|$.
^lagrange

> [!proof]- Proof (d 2.5)
> 1. Each $g$ lies in $gH$, since $e \in H$.
> 2. $h \mapsto gh$ is injective (cancellation), so $|gH| = |H|$.
> 3. If $gh = kh'$ with $h, h' \in H$, then $g = k h' h^{-1}$ with $h'h^{-1} \in H$, so every $gh''$ equals $k(h'h^{-1}h'') \in kH$: $gH \subseteq kH$. By symmetry $gH = kH$.
> 4. So the cosets are equal or disjoint, cover $G$ and have $|H|$ elements each: $|G| = (\text{number of cosets}) \cdot |H|$.
^p-lagrange

> [!thm] Corollary 4 (the order of an element divides the order of the group)
> In a finite group $G$, $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid |G|$ and $g^{|G|} = e$; with Theorem 2, $g^m = e$ exactly when $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid m$. A group of prime order $p$ is cyclic, and every element other than $e$ generates it.
^order-divides

> [!proof]- Proof
> 1. $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup with $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ elements (Theorem 2); apply Lagrange.
> 2. $|G|$ is a multiple of $\operatorname{ord}(g)$, so $g^{|G|} = e$ by Theorem 2.
> 3. If $|G| = p$ and $g \ne e$, then $\operatorname{ord}(g) \in \{1, p\}$ is not $1$, so $\langle g \rangle$ has $p$ elements: it is $G$.
^p-order-divides

> [!thm] Corollary 5 (Fermat's little theorem)
> If $p$ is prime and $p \nmid a$, then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$. For every integer $a$, $a^p \equiv a \pmod p$.
^fermat

> [!proof]- Proof
> 1. Every $k$ with $1 \le k \le p - 1$ is a unit mod $p$, so $|U(p)| = p - 1$.
> 2. Corollary 4 in $U(p)$.
> 3. Multiply by $a$; when $p \mid a$ both sides are $\equiv 0$.
^p-fermat

> [!thm] Corollary 6 (Euler's theorem)
> If $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, then $\operatorname{ord}_n(a) \mid \varphi(n)$ and $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$.
^euler

> [!proof]- Proof
> 1. Corollary 4 in $U(n)$, which has $\varphi(n)$ elements.
^p-euler

So a power of a unit depends only on the exponent mod $\operatorname{ord}_n(a)$, and $\varphi(n)$ is a safe modulus for the exponent. ^exponent-mod-order

> [!example] The units mod 15
> Find the order of each unit mod $15$. Is $U(15)$ cyclic?
^ex-u15

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: order
> 1. $U(15) = \{1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 11, 13, 14\}$, $\varphi(15) = 8$.
> 2. $2$: $2, 4, 8, 1$, order $4$. Likewise $7, 8, 13$ have order $4$; $4, 11, 14$ have order $2$; $1$ has order $1$.
> 3. No element has order $8$, so $U(15)$ is not cyclic. Lagrange allows orders dividing $8$; it does not promise one equal to $8$.

> [!example] A tower of threes
> Find the last two digits of $3^{3^{2026}}$.
^ex-tower

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: fermat-euler, order
> 1. Mod $100$: $3^5 = 243 \equiv 43$, $3^{10} \equiv 43^2 = 1849 \equiv 49$, $3^{20} \equiv 49^2 = 2401 \equiv 1$. So $\operatorname{ord}_{100}(3) \mid 20$, and the exponent matters only mod $20$.
> 2. Mod $20$: $3^4 = 81 \equiv 1$ and $2026 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$, so $3^{2026} \equiv 9 \pmod{20}$.
> 3. $3^9 = 19683$.
> Answer: $83$.

## Primitive roots

> [!thm] Theorem 7 (cyclic subgroups of fields)
> Let $G$ be a finite group of nonzero elements of a [field](alg-polynomials.md#^field-def) under multiplication. Then $G$ is cyclic. In particular $U(p)$ is cyclic for every prime $p$: some $g$, a **primitive root** mod $p$, has powers $g^0, \dots, g^{p-2}$ running through all nonzero residues.
^cyclic-units

> [!proof]- Proof (d 5)
> 1. Let $n = |G|$ and for $d \mid n$ let $N(d)$ be the number of elements of order $d$. Every order divides $n$ (Corollary 4), so $\sum_{d \mid n} N(d) = n$.
> 2. Suppose $N(d) > 0$ and $g$ has order $d$. Then $1, g, \dots, g^{d-1}$ are $d$ different roots of $x^d - 1$ in the field.
> 3. $x^d - 1$ has [at most $d$ roots](alg-polynomials.md#^field-roots) in a field, so every element of order $d$, being a root, is a power of $g$.
> 4. By Theorem 2, $g^k$ ($0 \le k < d$) has order $d$ exactly when $\gcd(d, k) = 1$: $\varphi(d)$ of them. So $N(d) \in \{0, \varphi(d)\}$.
> 5. $n = \sum_{d \mid n} N(d) \le \sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d) = n$, so $N(d) = \varphi(d)$ for every $d \mid n$. In particular $N(n) = \varphi(n) \ge 1$, and an element of order $n$ generates $G$.
^p-cyclic-units

> [!thm] Corollary 8 (power maps mod p)
> Let $p$ be prime and $g$ a primitive root mod $p$.
> (a) Each unit is $g^k$ for a $k$ unique mod $p - 1$.
> (b) $x^m \equiv 1$ has exactly $\gcd(m, p - 1)$ solutions mod $p$.
> (c) $x \mapsto x^m$ permutes $U(p)$ exactly when $\gcd(m, p - 1) = 1$.
> (d) For odd $p$, a unit $a$ is a square mod $p$ exactly when $a^{(p-1)/2} \equiv 1$, and otherwise $a^{(p-1)/2} \equiv -1$ (Euler's criterion). Exactly half the units are squares.
> (e) $-1$ is a square mod an odd prime $p$ exactly when $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
^power-maps

> [!proof]- Proof (d 3.5)
> 1. (a) $g$ has order $p - 1$; by Theorem 2 its powers $g^0, \dots, g^{p-2}$ are $p - 1$ different units, so all of them, and $g^k = g^j$ exactly when $k \equiv j \pmod{p-1}$.
> 2. (b) $(g^k)^m = 1$ exactly when $p - 1 \mid km$, which by cancelling is $\frac{p-1}{\gcd(m, p-1)} \mid k$: $\gcd(m, p-1)$ values of $k$ mod $p - 1$.
> 3. (c) $U(p)$ is abelian, so $x^m = y^m$ exactly when $(xy^{-1})^m = 1$. The map is injective, hence a permutation of the finite set, exactly when only $1$ has $m$-th power $1$; by (b) that is $\gcd(m, p-1) = 1$.
> 4. (d) The squares are the $g^{2j}$: the even indices, $(p-1)/2$ of them. And $(g^k)^{(p-1)/2} = 1$ exactly when $p - 1 \mid k(p-1)/2$, that is, $k$ even.
> 5. For odd $k$, $h = a^{(p-1)/2}$ has $h \ne 1$ and $h^2 = a^{p-1} = 1$, so $p \mid (h-1)(h+1)$ and $h \equiv -1$.
> 6. (e) Take $a = -1$ in (d): $(-1)^{(p-1)/2} = 1$ exactly when $(p-1)/2$ is even, and $1 \not\equiv -1$ for odd $p$.
^p-power-maps

Primitive roots exist mod $n$ exactly for $n = 1, 2, 4, p^k, 2p^k$ with $p$ an odd prime. That classification is not proved here; the [derive prompt on $p^2$](#^d-primitive-root-square) is its key step. ^primitive-root-moduli

> [!trap] Primitive roots do not pass up for free
> $U(8) = \{1, 3, 5, 7\}$ has no generator: every odd square is $\equiv 1 \pmod 8$. And $14$ is a primitive root mod $29$ but not mod $29^2$: $14^{28} \equiv 1 \pmod{841}$.
^trap-primitive-root

> [!example] Testing a primitive root
> Show that $2$ is a primitive root mod $101$.
^ex-primitive-101

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: order, primitive-root
> 1. $\operatorname{ord}_{101}(2) \mid 100$. If it is a proper divisor, it divides $100/2 = 50$ or $100/5 = 20$.
> 2. $2^{10} = 1024 \equiv 14$, $2^{20} \equiv 196 \equiv 95$, $2^{40} \equiv 95^2 \equiv 36$, $2^{50} \equiv 36 \cdot 14 = 504 \equiv 100 \equiv -1$.
> 3. Neither $2^{20}$ nor $2^{50}$ is $1$, so the order is $100$.

## Actions and counting orbits

> [!def] Group action, orbit, stabiliser
> An **action** of a group $G$ on a set $X$ assigns to each $g \in G$ and $x \in X$ an element $g \cdot x \in X$ with $e \cdot x = x$ and $g \cdot (h \cdot x) = (gh) \cdot x$. The **orbit** of $x$ is $G \cdot x = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\}$, its **stabiliser** is $G_x = \{g : g \cdot x = x\}$, and $\operatorname{Fix}(g) = \{x : g \cdot x = x\}$. "$y = g \cdot x$ for some $g$" is reflexive ($e$), symmetric ($g^{-1}$) and transitive (products), so the orbits split $X$.
^actions

Typical action: the rotations of a necklace of $n$ beads (the residues mod $n$ under $+$, rotation by $k$ moving bead $i$ to $i + k$) acting on its colourings. Two colourings in one orbit are the same necklace. ^actions-necklace

> [!thm] Theorem 9 (orbit–stabiliser)
> For a finite group acting on $X$, $G_x$ is a subgroup and $|G \cdot x| \cdot |G_x| = |G|$.
^orbit-stabiliser

> [!proof]- Proof (d 2.5)
> 1. $e \in G_x$; if $g, h$ fix $x$ so does $gh$; and $g^{-1} \cdot x = g^{-1} \cdot (g \cdot x) = x$.
> 2. $g \cdot x = h \cdot x$ exactly when $h^{-1}g \in G_x$, exactly when $gG_x = hG_x$ (step 3 of Lagrange's proof).
> 3. So $g \cdot x \mapsto gG_x$ is a bijection from the orbit to the cosets of $G_x$, of which there are $|G|/|G_x|$.
^p-orbit-stabiliser

> [!thm] Theorem 10 (Burnside's lemma)
> If a finite group $G$ acts on a finite set $X$, the number of orbits is $\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} |\operatorname{Fix}(g)|$.
^burnside

> [!proof]- Proof (d 3.5)
> 1. Count pairs $(g, x)$ with $g \cdot x = x$ [two ways](comb-binomial.md#^double-count): $\sum_g |\operatorname{Fix}(g)| = \sum_x |G_x|$.
> 2. By Theorem 9, $|G_x| = |G|/|G \cdot x|$.
> 3. Over the $|O|$ elements of one orbit $O$, the terms $1/|G \cdot x| = 1/|O|$ add to $1$. So $\sum_x |G_x| = |G| \cdot (\text{number of orbits})$.
^p-burnside

> [!example] Six-bead necklaces
> How many necklaces of $6$ beads can be made with $3$ colours, two being the same when one is a rotation of the other?
^ex-necklace

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: burnside, order
> 1. Rotation by $k$ moves bead $i$ to $i + k$; the beads split into cycles, each of length the additive order of $k$ mod $6$, which is $6/\gcd(6, k)$ (Theorem 2). So there are $\gcd(6, k)$ cycles.
> 2. A colouring is fixed exactly when it is constant on each cycle: $3^{\gcd(6,k)}$ colourings.
> 3. $k = 0, \dots, 5$: $3^6 + 3 + 3^2 + 3^3 + 3^2 + 3 = 780$.
> Answer: $780/6 = 130$.

## Problems

> [!problem] Cubing mod a prime (d 3)
> For how many primes $p < 100$ is the map $x \mapsto x^3$ a bijection of the residues mod $p$?
^inv-nt-cube-bijection

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: primitive-root
> 1. $0$ goes to $0$ and units go to units, so the map is a bijection exactly when it permutes $U(p)$, which by Corollary 8(c) is $\gcd(3, p - 1) = 1$: $p = 3$ or $p \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ (including $p = 2$).
> 2. The primes below $100$ that qualify: $2, 3, 5, 11, 17, 23, 29, 41, 47, 53, 59, 71, 83, 89$.
> Answer: $14$.

> [!problem] The repeating block of 1/511 (d 2.5)
> The decimal expansion of $1/511$ is periodic from the first digit. How long is its shortest repeating block?
^inv-nt-period-511

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: order, crt
> 1. For $\gcd(n, 10) = 1$, a block of length $d$ repeats exactly when $(10^d - 1)/n$ is an integer, the block's digits: so the least period is $\operatorname{ord}_n(10)$.
> 2. $511 = 7 \cdot 73$. $\operatorname{ord}_7(10) = 6$ ($10 \equiv 3$, a primitive root mod $7$). $73 \cdot 137 = 10001$, so $10^4 \equiv -1$ and $\operatorname{ord}_{73}(10) = 8$.
> 3. $10^d \equiv 1 \pmod{511}$ exactly when it holds mod $7$ and mod $73$, that is $6 \mid d$ and $8 \mid d$.
> Answer: $\operatorname{lcm}(6, 8) = 24$.

> [!problem] Black and white 3 × 3 grids (d 3)
> Each cell of a $3 \times 3$ grid is coloured black or white. How many colourings are there, counting two as the same when one is a rotation of the other by a multiple of $90°$?
^inv-nt-grid-rotations

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: burnside
> 1. Identity: $2^9 = 512$.
> 2. Quarter turns ($90°$ and $270°$): cycles are the centre, the four corners, the four edges: $2^3 = 8$ each.
> 3. Half turn: the centre and four pairs: $2^5 = 32$.
> Answer: $(512 + 8 + 8 + 32)/4 = 140$.

> [!problem] Exponents that fix every residue mod 66 (d 4)
> Find all positive integers $n$ such that $66$ divides $a^n - a$ for every integer $a$.
^inv-nt-fixed-exponent-66

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: primitive-root, fermat-euler, crt
> 1. $66 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 11$, pairwise coprime, so the condition holds exactly when it holds for $p = 2, 3, 11$.
> 2. For $p \mid a$ it is automatic. For $p \nmid a$, cancel $a$: the condition is $a^{n-1} \equiv 1$ for every unit $a$ mod $p$.
> 3. A primitive root $g$ mod $p$ forces $p - 1 \mid n - 1$; conversely $p - 1 \mid n - 1$ suffices by Fermat.
> 4. So $1$, $2$ and $10$ divide $n - 1$.
> Answer: $n \equiv 1 \pmod{10}$.

> [!problem] Power sums mod a prime (d 4.5)
> Let $p$ be an odd prime. For which $m \ge 1$ does $p$ divide $1^m + 2^m + \dots + (p - 1)^m$?
^inv-nt-power-sums

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: primitive-root
> 1. With a primitive root $g$, the sum is $S \equiv \sum_{k=0}^{p-2} g^{km}$.
> 2. If $p - 1 \mid m$, every term is $1$ and $S \equiv p - 1 \equiv -1$.
> 3. Otherwise $h = g^m \not\equiv 1$, and $(h - 1)S \equiv h^{p-1} - 1 \equiv 0$. Since $h - 1$ is a unit, $S \equiv 0$.
> Answer: exactly when $p - 1 \nmid m$.

## Derive

> [!derive] Prove that a group in which $g^2 = e$ for every $g$ is abelian.
^d-exponent-two

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: alg-groups/group-basics
> 1. Each element is its own inverse, so $ab = (ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1} = ba$.

> [!derive] Prove that every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic. (d 3)
^d-subgroup-cyclic

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: alg-groups/elt-order
> 1. Let $G = \langle g \rangle$ and $H \ne \{e\}$ a subgroup; let $m \ge 1$ be least with $g^m \in H$ (inverses make some positive power lie in $H$).
> 2. For $g^k \in H$ write $k = qm + r$, $0 \le r < m$: $g^r = g^k (g^m)^{-q} \in H$, so $r = 0$. Hence $H = \langle g^m \rangle$.

> [!derive] Let $n > 1$, let $p$ be the least prime factor of $n$, and let $p \nmid a$. Prove that if $a^n \equiv 1 \pmod p$ then $a \equiv 1 \pmod p$. (d 3)
^d-least-prime-factor

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: order, fermat-euler, bezout
> 1. $d = \operatorname{ord}_p(a)$ divides $n$ and $p - 1$.
> 2. Every prime factor of $p - 1$ is less than $p$, so none divides $n$: $\gcd(n, p - 1) = 1$, and $d = 1$.

> [!derive] Let $p$ be an odd prime and $g$ a primitive root mod $p$. Prove that $g$ or $g + p$ is a primitive root mod $p^2$. (d 5)
^d-primitive-root-square

> [!solution]- Solution
> methods: order, nt-padic/taylor-int
> 1. For $h \equiv g \pmod p$, $d = \operatorname{ord}_{p^2}(h)$ divides $\varphi(p^2) = p(p-1)$, and $h^d \equiv 1 \pmod p$ gives $p - 1 \mid d$. So $d \in \{p - 1, p(p-1)\}$.
> 2. If $g^{p-1} \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}$, $g$ works.
> 3. Otherwise, by the [first-order expansion](nt-padic.md#^taylor-int), $(g + p)^{p-1} \equiv g^{p-1} + (p-1)g^{p-2}p \equiv 1 - p\,g^{p-2} \pmod{p^2}$, and $p \nmid g^{p-2}$, so this is not $1$: $g + p$ works.
